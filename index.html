Lesson Notes

What is an inference?

An inference is a smart guess you make using clues from the text plus what you already know. Authors do not tell you everything. Good readers figure out the rest.
Una inferencia es una conclusión que haces con pistas del texto y lo que ya sabes.

Text clue+What I know=Inference
  1. Read the question. What does it want me to figure out? A feeling? A reason? What happens next?
  2. Find a clue. Look for words about actions, faces, voices, or the setting. Put your finger on it.
  3. Add what you know. When people act like this in real life, what does it usually mean?
  4. Make the inference. Say it with the frame, then check: does every clue still make sense?
It says: The text says “___.”
I know: I know that when ___, it usually means ___.
And so: So I can infer that ___.

I DOWatch the Coach Think Aloud

Sentence: Mr. Torres looked at the dark clouds, then quickly carried his lawn chairs into the garage.

Question: What does Mr. Torres think is going to happen?

  1. Clue: “looked at the dark clouds” and “quickly carried his lawn chairs into the garage.”
  2. I know: Dark clouds usually mean a storm. People put things away so the wind won’t blow them.
  3. Inference: So I can infer that Mr. Torres thinks a bad storm is coming soon.
  4. Check: The word “quickly” fits too. He is in a hurry because the storm is close.

Notice: the sentence never says “storm.” I figured it out.

Teacher coaching guide · Station 2
Look-fors
  • Students point to a specific phrase before answering.
  • They say “I know…” with a real-life connection, not a restated clue.
  • Their inference goes beyond what the text says directly.
Common misconceptions
  • Repeating a detail and calling it an inference. Ask: “Did the author say that? Then it’s not an inference.”
  • Guessing with no clue. Ask: “Which words made you think that?”
  • Using only one clue when two clues point the same way.
Coaching prompts
  • “Show me the words.”
  • “What does that usually mean in real life?”
  • “Does your idea still fit ¶4?”
  • “¿Qué pista te dio esa idea?”
Differentiate
  • Support: Pair students, allow L1 discussion, and reveal the first hint for them.
  • Core: Use the three frames as written.
  • Challenge: Infer the theme of the story and support it with two clues from different paragraphs.

Answer key: We do: 1-B, 2-C, 3-A, 4-D, 5-B. Quick infer: 1-C, 2-A, 3-B. Challenge sample theme: Family and preparation matter more than things (¶3 Grandfather acts early; ¶5 Mateo packs only the family photo). You-do sample: The text says Mateo took only the family photo. I know people save things that matter most to them. So I can infer that Mateo cares most about his family being together and safe.

LESSONWatch the Teacher: Mr. Okafor

Put on headphones and press Play lesson. The teacher will stop at “Your turn” so you can practice with a partner. Captions are always on.

Lesson Notes

What is a complex sentence?

A complex sentence joins two ideas with a connecting word (a subordinating conjunction). It shows how the ideas are related. This makes your writing sound more academic, and ELPA21 writing scores reward it.
Una oración compleja une dos ideas con una palabra de enlace como “because” (porque) o “although” (aunque).

WordShowsEspañolExample
becausereasonporqueWe left early because the roads were flooding.
althoughcontrastaunqueAlthough the rain stopped, the wind was still strong.
whentimecuandoWhen the siren sounds, go to the hallway.
ifconditionsiIf the power goes out, use a flashlight.
so thatpurposepara queWe charged our phones so that we could call for help.
Comma rule. If the connecting word comes first, put a comma after the first idea: Because the roads flooded, we stayed home. If it comes in the middle, you usually don’t need a comma: We stayed home because the roads flooded.
  1. Read both sentences. What is the relationship: reason, contrast, time, condition, or purpose?
  2. Choose the connecting word that matches the relationship.
  3. Join and cut. Put the sentences together and remove extra words or repeated names.
  4. Check the comma and the capital letter. Read it aloud to hear whether it sounds complete.

I DOWatch the Coach Combine

Sentence A: The store ran out of water. Sentence B: Many families were preparing for the hurricane.

  1. Relationship: Why did the store run out? Because families were preparing. That is a reason.
  2. Word: A reason means because.
  3. Join: The store ran out of water because many families were preparing for the hurricane.
  4. Check: “Because” is in the middle, so no comma. Moving it to the front gives: Because many families were preparing for the hurricane, the store ran out of water. Now it needs a comma.

Choose the word that shows the correct relationship. Use Coach me if you need it.

Challenge: Fix the Choppy Paragraph

Rewrite this paragraph using four complex sentences. Try at least one of these harder connecting words: unless, even though, since, before, after.

The storm was coming. My family went to the store. The store was crowded. We found water. My dad bought batteries. The power might go out. We got home. It started to rain. My sister was scared. We stayed calm.

Connecting words used: 0 / 4
Teacher coaching guide · Station 3
Look-fors
  • Students name the relationship before choosing a word.
  • The comma is placed correctly when the sentence opens with the clause.
  • They remove repeated subjects when joining (“The family… The family…”).
Common errors
  • Fragments: “Because the wind was strong.” Ask: “Because… what happened?”
  • Double connectors: “Although it rained, but we went.” Spanish speakers may transfer aunque… pero. Keep only one.
  • Using because for everything.
Coaching prompts
  • “Is this a reason, a contrast, or a time?”
  • “Read it aloud. Does it sound finished?”
  • “Where does the comma go if you flip it?”
  • “¿Qué relación hay entre las dos ideas?”
Differentiate
  • Support: Give students only because and when first, and accept the connector in the middle position.
  • Core: Use all five words.
  • Challenge: Add unless, even though and since, and start two sentences with the clause.

Answer key (We do): 1-B because, 2-A although, 3-C when, 4-D so that, 5-B if. You-do samples: (1) Although the storm was far away, families started preparing. (2) We moved to the hallway when the wind got stronger. (3) If the power goes out, we will use flashlights. (4) Grandpa boarded the windows so that the glass would not break. Challenge sample: Since the storm was coming, my family went to the store. Even though the store was crowded, we found water. My dad bought batteries because the power might go out. After we got home, it started to rain.
